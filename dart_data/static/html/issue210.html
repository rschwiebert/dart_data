Erratum Record: Issue #210

1. Context & Claim
Subject / Target Issue 210 (Ring 42)
Target Citation T. Y. Lam (2007). Exercises in modules and rings. Problem Books in Mathematics, Springer, Exercise 8.13, p. 211.
Claim: Let $S = k\langle\langle x, y\rangle\rangle$ be the power series ring in two noncommuting variables. Then $R = S/(y^2, yx)$ has the representation $R = k[[\bar{x}]] \oplus k[[\bar{x}]]\bar{y}$.
Statement Established The algebraic two-sided ideal $I = (y^2, yx)$ generated by $y^2$ and $yx$ in $S$ is strictly smaller than the ideal of all series whose terms contain $y^2$ or $yx$; consequently, $S/I \not\cong k[[\bar{x}]] \oplus k[[\bar{x}]]\bar{y}$. However, replacing $I$ with its $(x, y)$-adic completion (topological closure) $\bar{I}$ yields exactly the desired representation $R = S/\bar{I} = k[[\bar{x}]] \oplus k[[\bar{x}]]\bar{y}$, and Lam's proof that $R$ is local, right Kasch, and not left Kasch goes through without alteration.

Background & Statement of the Problem

Let $k$ be a field, and let $S = k\langle\langle x, y\rangle\rangle$ denote the Magnus algebra (the ring of formal power series in two noncommuting indeterminates $x, y$). Elements of $S$ are formal series: $$ s = \sum_{w \in \langle x, y\rangle} c_w w \quad (c_w \in k), $$ where $\langle x, y\rangle$ denotes the free monoid of words in $x$ and $y$. The ring $S$ is naturally complete under the $(x, y)$-adic topology defined by the filtration of powers of the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{n} = (x, y)$ of series with zero constant term.

In Exercise 8.13 (p. 211), Lam presents an example of a local ring that is right Kasch but not left Kasch:

Ex. 8.13. Find an example of a local ring $R$ that is right Kasch but not left Kasch.

Solution. Let $k$ be any field, and $S$ be the ring of power series in two (noncommuting) variables $x, y$. We take $R$ to be $S / (y^2, y x)$, which is a local ring with unique maximal left (resp. right) ideal $\mathfrak{m}=(x, y) / (y^2, y x)$. In working with $R$, it is convenient to use the representation $$ R = k[[\bar{x}]] \oplus k[[\bar{x}]] \bar{y}. $$
However, in noncommutative power series rings, the ordinary algebraic ideal $I = (y^2, yx)$ generated by $y^2$ and $yx$ contains only finite sums of elements of the form $a y^2 b$ and $c yx d$. As we show below, $I$ does not contain all formal series whose individual monomials contain $y^2$ or $yx$. As a result, the quotient $S/I$ contains elements that cannot be written in the form $f(\bar{x}) + g(\bar{x})\bar{y}$, so the asserted direct-sum representation fails for $S/I$.

Failure of the Algebraic Quotient

Proposition 1. Let $I = (y^2, yx)$ be the two-sided algebraic ideal of $S = k\langle\langle x, y\rangle\rangle$ generated by $y^2$ and $yx$. The element: $$ f = \sum_{n=1}^\infty x^n y^2 x^n \in S $$ does not belong to $I$, even though each of its constituent monomials $x^n y^2 x^n$ belongs to $I$. Consequently, $S/I \neq k[[\bar{x}]] \oplus k[[\bar{x}]]\bar{y}$.

Proof. By definition of the two-sided ideal generated by $y^2$ and $yx$, every element $h \in I$ is a finite sum: $$ h = \sum_{i=1}^p a_i y^2 b_i + \sum_{j=1}^q c_j yx d_j \qquad (p, q \in \mathbb{N},\; a_i, b_i, c_j, d_j \in S). $$ Suppose for the sake of contradiction that $f \in I$, so that: $$ \sum_{n=1}^\infty x^n y^2 x^n = \sum_{i=1}^p a_i y^2 b_i + \sum_{j=1}^q c_j yx d_j. $$ Let $W \subset S$ be the $k$-subspace consisting of all series whose nonzero coefficients occur only on monomials of the form $x^k y^2 x^m$ with $k, m \ge 0$. Let $\pi_W: S \to W$ be the canonical $k$-linear projection sending a series $\sum_{w} c_w w$ to $\sum_{k, m \ge 0} c_{x^k y^2 x^m} x^k y^2 x^m$.

We evaluate the image of each term under $\pi_W$:

  1. Every monomial in $c_j yx d_j$ contains the subword $yx$. However, no monomial $x^k y^2 x^m$ contains $yx$, because all occurrences of $x$ appear strictly to the left or strictly to the right of $y^2$, and $x$ never directly follows $y$. Thus, $\pi_W\left(\sum_{j=1}^q c_j yx d_j\right) = 0$.
  2. For any product $a_i y^2 b_i$, write $a_i = \alpha_i(x) + a'_i$ and $b_i = \beta_i(x) + b'_i$, where $\alpha_i(x), \beta_i(x) \in k[[x]]$ are the terms containing no occurrence of $y$, and $a'_i, b'_i$ consist of series whose terms each contain at least one $y$. Then in the expansion: $$ a_i y^2 b_i = \alpha_i(x) y^2 \beta_i(x) + \alpha_i(x) y^2 b'_i + a'_i y^2 \beta_i(x) + a'_i y^2 b'_i, $$ every term in the last three summands contains at least three factors of $y$. Hence none of those terms can have the form $x^k y^2 x^m$. Therefore: $$ \pi_W(a_i y^2 b_i) = \alpha_i(x) y^2 \beta_i(x). $$
Applying $\pi_W$ to both sides of the assumed equality yields: $$ \sum_{n=1}^\infty x^n y^2 x^n = \sum_{i=1}^p \alpha_i(x) y^2 \beta_i(x), \quad \text{where } \alpha_i(x), \beta_i(x) \in k[[x]]. $$ Now, identify $W$ with the vector space of $\mathbb{N}_0 \times \mathbb{N}_0$ infinite matrices over $k$ by associating the monomial $x^k y^2 x^m$ with the matrix unit $E_{k, m}$. Under this identification:
  • The left-hand side $\sum_{n=1}^\infty x^n y^2 x^n$ corresponds to the infinite diagonal matrix $\sum_{n=1}^\infty E_{n, n}$, which has infinite rank.
  • Writing $\alpha_i(x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty u_{i,k} x^k$ and $\beta_i(x) = \sum_{m=0}^\infty v_{i,m} x^m$, each term $\alpha_i(x) y^2 \beta_i(x)$ corresponds to the rank-1 outer product $\mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T = (u_{i,k} v_{i,m})_{k,m \ge 0}$.
  • Consequently, the right-hand side $\sum_{i=1}^p \alpha_i(x) y^2 \beta_i(x)$ corresponds to a matrix of rank at most $p < \infty$.
This is a contradiction, since an infinite-rank matrix cannot be a finite sum of rank-1 matrices. Therefore, $f \notin I$.

Moreover, because $f$ consists purely of monomials containing $y^2$, any representation $f \equiv f_0(\bar{x}) + g_0(\bar{x})\bar{y} \pmod I$ would require $f - f_0(x) - g_0(x)y \in I$, which under $\pi_W$ would force $f = 0$, an impossibility. Hence $S/I \not\cong k[[\bar{x}]] \oplus k[[\bar{x}]]\bar{y}$. $\blacksquare$

The Repair via Topological Completion

Let $\bar{I}$ denote the closure of $I = (y^2, yx)$ in $S$ with respect to the $(x, y)$-adic topology: $$ \bar{I} = \bigcap_{N=1}^\infty \left( I + \mathfrak{n}^N \right). $$ Equivalently, $\bar{I}$ consists of all formal power series in $S$ whose support contains only words $w$ that have $y^2$ or $yx$ as a subword: $$ \bar{I} = \left\{ \sum_{w \in \langle x, y\rangle} c_w w \in S \;\middle|\; c_w = 0 \text{ whenever } w \text{ does not contain } y^2 \text{ or } yx \right\}. $$

Proposition 2. Let $R = S / \bar{I}$. Then $R$ admits the internal direct-sum decomposition: $$ R = k[[\bar{x}]] \oplus k[[\bar{x}]]\bar{y}. $$

Proof. Let $w \in \langle x, y\rangle$ be a word that does not contain $y^2$ or $yx$ as a subword:

  • If $w$ contains no occurrence of $y$, then $w = x^k$ for some $k \ge 0$.
  • If $w$ contains an occurrence of $y$, any letter immediately following $y$ cannot be $x$ (since $yx$ is forbidden) and cannot be $y$ (since $y^2$ is forbidden). Thus, $y$ can only appear as the final letter of $w$. Furthermore, $w$ cannot contain an earlier $y$, since that earlier $y$ would be followed by some letter. Thus $w = x^k y$ for some $k \ge 0$.
Hence, the set of words avoiding both $y^2$ and $yx$ is precisely $\{x^k\}_{k \ge 0} \cup \{x^k y\}_{k \ge 0}$.

Given any series $s = \sum_{w} c_w w \in S$, we can uniquely partition it into: $$ s = \sum_{k=0}^\infty c_{x^k} x^k + \left(\sum_{k=0}^\infty c_{x^k y} x^k\right) y + \sum_{w \text{ contains } y^2 \text{ or } yx} c_w w. $$ The third sum lies in $\bar{I}$, while the first two sums define series in $k[[x]]$ and $k[[x]]y$, respectively. Moreover, if $f(x) + g(x)y \in \bar{I}$ for some $f, g \in k[[x]]$, then because neither $x^k$ nor $x^k y$ contains $y^2$ or $yx$, all coefficients of $f$ and $g$ must be zero, so $f = g = 0$.

It follows that $R = S/\bar{I} = k[[\bar{x}]] \oplus k[[\bar{x}]]\bar{y}$ as $k$-vector spaces. $\blacksquare$

Verification of Lam's Kasch Properties for $R = S/\bar{I}$

With the representation $R = k[[\bar{x}]] \oplus k[[\bar{x}]]\bar{y}$ established, every element $r \in R$ is uniquely written as $r = f(\bar{x}) + g(\bar{x})\bar{y}$ with $f, g \in k[[\bar{x}]]$. Because $\bar{y}\bar{x} = 0$ and $\bar{y}^2 = 0$, we have: $$ (f_1(\bar{x}) + g_1(\bar{x})\bar{y})(f_2(\bar{x}) + g_2(\bar{x})\bar{y}) = f_1(\bar{x})f_2(\bar{x}) + \big(f_1(\bar{x})g_2(\bar{x}) + g_1(\bar{x})f_2(0)\big)\bar{y}. $$

1. $R$ is a local ring:
An element $r = f(\bar{x}) + g(\bar{x})\bar{y}$ is invertible if and only if $f(0) \ne 0$. Indeed, if $f(0) \ne 0$, then $f(\bar{x})$ is a unit in $k[[\bar{x}]]$, and: $$ (f(\bar{x}) + g(\bar{x})\bar{y})^{-1} = f(\bar{x})^{-1} - f(\bar{x})^{-1} g(\bar{x}) f(0)^{-1}\bar{y}. $$ The non-units form the unique maximal two-sided ideal $\mathfrak{m} = \bar{x}k[[\bar{x}]] \oplus k[[\bar{x}]]\bar{y} = (\bar{x}, \bar{y})$.

2. $R$ is not left Kasch:
Suppose $r = f(\bar{x}) + g(\bar{x})\bar{y} \in \operatorname{ann}_r(\bar{x})$. Then: $$ \bar{x}r = \bar{x}f(\bar{x}) + \bar{x}g(\bar{x})\bar{y} = 0. $$ By direct-sum uniqueness, $\bar{x}f(\bar{x}) = 0$ and $\bar{x}g(\bar{x}) = 0$ in $k[[\bar{x}]]$. Since $k[[\bar{x}]]$ is an integral domain, $f = g = 0$, so $\operatorname{ann}_r(\bar{x}) = 0$. Consequently: $$ \operatorname{ann}_r(\mathfrak{m}) \subseteq \operatorname{ann}_r(\bar{x}) = 0. $$ For a local ring with unique simple module $V = R/\mathfrak{m}$, the left module ${_R V}$ embeds into ${_R R}$ if and only if $\operatorname{ann}_r(\mathfrak{m}) \ne 0$ (Lam, Lectures on Modules and Rings, (8.27)). Since $\operatorname{ann}_r(\mathfrak{m}) = 0$, ${_R V}$ does not embed into ${_R R}$, so $R$ is not left Kasch.

3. $R$ is right Kasch:
Consider $0 \ne \bar{y} \in R$. For any element $\bar{x}a(\bar{x}) + b(\bar{x})\bar{y} \in \mathfrak{m}$, we have: $$ \bar{y}(\bar{x}a(\bar{x}) + b(\bar{x})\bar{y}) = (\bar{y}\bar{x})a(\bar{x}) + \bar{y}b(\bar{x})\bar{y} = 0 + b(0)\bar{y}^2 = 0. $$ Thus, $0 \ne \bar{y} \in \operatorname{ann}_\ell(\mathfrak{m})$. Moreover, for any $r = f(\bar{x}) + g(\bar{x})\bar{y} \in R$, $\bar{y}r = f(0)\bar{y}$, so $\bar{y}r = 0 \iff f(0) = 0 \iff r \in \mathfrak{m}$. Hence $\operatorname{ann}_r(\bar{y}) = \mathfrak{m}$, which implies that the right ideal $\bar{y}R$ is isomorphic to $R/\mathfrak{m}$: $$ \bar{y}R \cong R / \operatorname{ann}_r(\bar{y}) = R / \mathfrak{m}. $$ Therefore, the unique simple right module $(R/\mathfrak{m})_R$ embeds into $R_R$ as the minimal right ideal $\bar{y}R$. In particular, $R$ is right Kasch. $\blacksquare$

Conclusion

Lam's intended construction and all of his subsequent mathematical assertions are completely correct once the ideal $(y^2, yx)$ is understood as the $(x, y)$-adically closed ideal $\overline{(y^2, yx)}$ in the Magnus algebra $k\langle\langle x, y\rangle\rangle$.
